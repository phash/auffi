Free remote support: where the free tiers actually end

Almost every remote-access tool advertises a free version. The difference is not the price but the condition attached: free for private use, paid for business is a very different offer from free with no such distinction. This page sorts which tool falls into which category — and how to spot a block before it lands in the middle of a session.

The trap: when free suddenly reads as commercial

The usual breaking point is not the invoice but the middle of a job: the session drops after a few minutes and a commercial-use notice appears. What triggers it is not a bill but a behaviour pattern — many different target machines, access during office hours, corporate networks in the background.

The uncomfortable part is how blurry that line is. Volunteering at a club or looking after the family's machines on the side can land you in the same detection. You can appeal, but it takes time — and the session ended anyway. The detail is under TeamViewer commercial use.

How to tell that free will stay free

  • Read the licence, not the pricing page. Under AGPL, GPL or Apache the use cannot be narrowed to "private only" after the fact.
  • Look for a device cap. Free tiers from commercial vendors usually cover only a handful of endpoints.
  • Check whether both sides need an account. An account requirement is not a fee, but it is a lock-in — and a real hurdle for one-off help.
  • Ask whether self-hosting is allowed. If you can run the service yourself, you are independent of anyone's pricing decisions.

The honest test is what happens if the vendor changes its pricing tomorrow. With an open-source tool that you may self-host, the answer is: nothing, you keep running your own instance. With a free tier, the answer is that you do not negotiate — you get informed.

Free for whom? Three typical cases

Family and neighbours. Purely private and plainly uncontroversial — practically every tool stays inside its free tier here. The only real difference is the hurdle: if you first have to talk someone through an installation, you lose more time than the actual help costs.

Clubs and volunteering. The trickiest case. Formally not commercial, yet behaviourally almost indistinguishable from it: many different machines, often during the day. That is exactly the pattern detection keys on. If you help out regularly, a tool that draws no such line saves a lot of nerves.

Freelancers and small businesses. Unambiguously commercial — with TeamViewer and AnyDesk a licence is due as soon as the usage is detected. If that is not in the budget, you need a tool that includes commercial use from the start rather than policing it.

Frequently asked questions

Which remote support is free for commercial use too?

Auffi and RustDesk are both open source under AGPL-3.0 and draw no line between private and commercial use. With TeamViewer and AnyDesk the free tier ends precisely where money is being made — they detect commercial patterns and then require a licence.

Why does my free session get cut after a few minutes?

That is usually not a fault but commercial-use detection. Connections to many different machines, access during office hours or corporate domains on the network all count as evidence. The session is then ended after a few minutes and a licence notice appears.

Is free remote support secure?

Price says nothing about security. What matters is transport encryption, how long an access code stays valid, whether every connection has to be confirmed actively, and whether the source can be audited. A free open-source tool can be audited; a paid proprietary one cannot.

What does free mean for open-source tools?

That no licence fee applies and that you may additionally run the software yourself. It does not mean operating it is free: self-hosting costs a server and your time. Auffi's public service is free to use and is funded by donations.

Where is the catch in free tiers, typically?

Usually in one of four places: commercial use is excluded, the number of managed devices is capped, only a single technician can work at a time, or an account requirement ties both sides to one vendor. Checking those four before a tool reaches daily use saves the unpleasant surprise later.
